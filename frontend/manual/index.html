Hull Anomaly Annotation Manual

Ship hull plating · semantic segmentation · 8 classes · working resolution 2592 × 1944 — back to the annotation app

This page teaches annotation judgment: what to label, which class it is, and where the boundaries go. Read the quick start, then the common mistakes. Keep the colour reference open while you work (button, bottom right).

Quick start — the 10 rules that matter most#

  1. Corroded surface = Rust. Run-off from it = Dirt. If the surface itself is corroded, it is Rust. If it is discolouration that ran down from corrosion, it is Dirt 1 (heavy) or Dirt 2 (light). Manual p11 · FP-1
  2. Sweep for rust before you finish — then check every rust mask really is corrosion. Both directions cost rework. FP-2
  3. Blackish or metallic exposure = Steel. Grey or white = AC. Both definitions include "white", so white alone decides nothing. Manual p12–13 · FP-3
  4. Peeled, but neither AC nor steel = AF deterioration. Manual p14
  5. Follow the anomaly's actual edge. No blobs, no bounding rectangles. FP-4
  6. Fill each class to its own extent. Do not let one mask swallow its neighbours. FP-5
  7. Large gap → separate regions. Do not enclose them as one blob. Ruling JP-3
  8. Classes may overlap — overlay them. Ruling JP-9
  9. Water drips, faint whitening, faded colour and normal AF wear are NOT dirt. Manual p17
  10. Misalignment tolerance is 2 px on the outside. Manual p5 · how to read this

Common mistakes to avoid#

Five habits show up again and again in our work. Each one below is a habit to change, not a comment on any single file. Learn to spot these five in your own work in progress.

How to read the client's markup Red frame = the thing being pointed at. Yellow frame = an area we annotated wrongly. Where you see our own masks, the fill colour is the class we assigned — check it against the class list.

FP-1 · We have been labelling rust as dirt#

What we have been doing: filling corroded surface with Dirt 1 (green) because it looks like a brown stain.
What we must do: ask what the surface is doing. Corroded metal → Rust. Discolouration that ran down from corrosion → Dirt 1 (heavy) or Dirt 2 (light).

Why this happens: rust and its run-off are the same colour, so colour cannot settle it. The manual's own wording is the tell — Rust is rust without rust leachate. The rust is the source; the streak is what leaked out of it.

FP-2 · We have been missing rust — and calling things rust that are not#

What we have been doing: leaving real rust unlabelled, and at the same time spreading rust masks over surface that is not corroded.
What we must do: two passes. First sweep the whole image for rust you missed. Then re-check each rust mask you drew and confirm the surface under it is actually corroded.

Why this happens: small or faint rust reads as background and gets skipped; conversely, any orange-brown tone reads as "rust" and gets over-claimed. This is one habit with two directions, and the client flagged both on the same image.

FP-3 · We have been defaulting to AC when an exposure looks pale#

What we have been doing: reaching for Exposed AC paint (cyan) whenever paint has come off and the area underneath looks light.
What we must do: check the tone. Blackish or metallic → Exposed steel plate. Grey or white → Exposed AC paint.
Why the definitions trap us The manual defines exposed steel as "white or blackish" and exposed AC as "white or greyish". Both include white — so white alone decides nothing. The decision is made by the other tone: is there blackish, metallic colour, or is it grey?

FP-4 · We have been drawing loose blobs around anomalies#

What we have been doing: enclosing an anomaly in one smooth shape, swallowing the sound surface between its parts.
What we must do: follow the actual edge. Where sound paint shows between two parts of an anomaly, leave it unfilled.

Why this happens: a smooth outline is much faster to draw and feels "close enough". At 2592 × 1944 with a 2 px tolerance, it is not. The three images below are the same region — the client supplied the granularity they expect.

FP-5 · We have been letting one mask swallow the area#

What we have been doing: covering a busy region with one large mask, so rust and dirt sitting side by side end up inside a single class.
What we must do: fill each class to its own extent. Where rust and dirt are both present, separate them. Keep the same level of detail across the whole image.

Why this happens: where two classes touch, drawing one boundary is easier than drawing two. But the classes mean different things to the model, so a merged mask teaches it the wrong thing.

1 · Rust spots

rgb(217, 83, 25)

Definition: areas of rust that do not contain rust leachate. Manual p11

Looks like: the metal surface itself has corroded — rough, orange-brown, often along seams, weld lines and edges.

Commonly confused with — Dirt 1 / Dirt 2 Ask: is the surface corroded, or is this a stain that ran down from corrosion? Corroded surface → Rust. Streak below it → Dirt 1 if heavy, Dirt 2 if light. See FP-1.
Manual vs later correction The manual says rust stains are Dirt (p15–16). The client's later review found we had used that rule to label corroded surface as dirt. Do this: the streak rule applies only to the run-off. The corroded area itself is always Rust.

2 · Exposed Steel Plate Area

rgb(30, 90, 255)

Definition: areas where the paint has come away revealing the steel plate — white or blackish areas. Manual p12

Looks like: bare plate. Often dark, grey-blue or metallic; may have rust in the recesses.

Commonly confused with — Exposed AC paint Blackish or metallic → Steel. Grey or white → AC. Both definitions include "white", so white alone decides nothing. See FP-3.

3 · Exposed areas of AC paint

rgb(15, 255, 255)

Definition: areas where the paint has peeled away revealing white or greyish surfaces. Manual p13

Looks like: the anticorrosive layer under the topcoat — pale, flat grey or white, not metallic.

Commonly confused with — Exposed steel plate If you can see blackish or metallic plate, it is Steel. Grey/white under-paint is AC. See FP-3 — this was one of our two most costly confusions.

Client ruling JP-7 confirms a white-looking painted area in their example is AC.

4 · AF paint deterioration / peeling

rgb(255, 19, 166)

Definition: areas where paint has peeled off but which do not match the AC or steel exposure classes. Manual p14

Looks like: the antifouling topcoat breaking up, flaking or "melting" away, without exposing grey under-paint or bare plate.

Decide in this order Bare metallic plate → Steel. Grey/white under-paint → AC. Peeled but neither of those → AF.

Rulings: JP-2 (a "melting" appearance is AF), EN-6 (a scattered pattern is AF).

5 · Dirt 1 — heavy

rgb(100, 212, 19)

Definition: rubber marks, heavy rust streaks, oil stains, soap/wash residue. Manual p10, p15

  • Dirt blackened by rubber marks.
  • Rust stains (the run-off, not the corroded surface).
  • Stains left by oil or other substances dripping.
  • Paint drip / run marks — ruling EN-7
  • Residue left after washing.
Commonly confused with — Rust A streak running down from corrosion is Dirt 1. The corroded surface that produced it is Rust. See FP-1.

6 · Dirt 2 — light

rgb(255, 215, 0)

Definition: light rust streaks, water stains / water scale. Manual p10, p16

  • Stains with only a small amount of rust.
  • Water scale / water spots. — rulings EN-4, EN-9
  • A stain in a colour different from the hull's paint.
Dirt 1 or Dirt 2? Heavy and dark → Dirt 1. Faint and light → Dirt 2. If an oily-looking mark turns out to be water scale, it is Dirt 2 — the client ruled this twice.
These are NOT dirt
  • Areas where water has dripped, or that merely look stained.
  • Areas that have become faintly whitish overall.
  • Areas where the colour has faded.
  • Normal AF wear, where wear is widespread.

7 · Residual marine organisms and other deposits

rgb(183, 70, 255)

Definition: white round spots; bright white areas. Manual p18

Ruling — separate the white portion Where an organism has an inner white portion, that white part is the adhered deposit. Separate them. Ruling JP-4

8 · Sound areas

rgb(0, 0, 0)

Definition: areas with no abnormality, including normal wear of AF paint. Manual p10, p19

Areas that appear indented, as though the paint might have peeled, but where it has not actually peeled, are sound.

Ruling EN-1: a sound area sitting inside another class may be annotated as Sound on top of it — send the larger class to the back so the smaller shape stays in front.

Annotation technique#

Granularity — follow the shape#

Follow the actual edge of the anomaly. Do not draw a rectangle or a smooth blob that contains it. Where sound paint shows between parts of an anomaly, leave that paint unfilled.

Work at the resolution the job is delivered at — 2592 × 1944. That is what makes "fine enough" fine enough. Zoom in; an outline that looks tight at fit-to-screen is usually a slab at full size.

Compare the three-image sequence in FP-4 — it is the client's own statement of the granularity they expect.

The 2 px tolerance — "on the outside"#

Misalignment tolerance is 2 px on the outside. Manual p5 Your boundary may sit up to 2 px outside the true edge of the anomaly. It is a small allowance for edge precision — not licence to round off a shape.

Separate regions across a large gap#

Client ruling JP-3 Separate the sections where there is a large gap. Do not enclose two separated areas in one mask just because they are the same class.

Overlapping classes#

Client ruling JP-9 Overlapping is allowed — overlay them. If an area is both peeling and dirty, you do not have to choose. Send the larger class to the back (Shift + B) so the smaller shape stays visible in front. Ruling EN-1 applies the same technique to a sound area sitting inside a larger class.

Scattered patterns#

Where an anomaly appears as many small scattered spots, follow the spots — do not sweep one mask across the whole field. The client classed a scattered pattern of this kind as AF deterioration, and asked that we cover as little sound area as possible. Ruling EN-6

Numerals and markings#

Client ruling JP-6 Numerals and markings painted on the hull do require annotation. Do not skip a draft mark or a painted character because it is "not part of the hull".

Cracks#

Annotate cracks at the level of detail the client's example shows. Ruling JP-1

Reading an exposure: steel or AC?#

Return to the draft-mark image in FP-3. Walk it in this order:

  1. Paint has come off — so this is the Steel / AC / AF branch.
  2. Is bare metallic plate visible? Yes — grey-blue, metallic, with rust sitting in the recesses. → Exposed steel plate.
  3. Any grey/white under-paint at the margins, without metal? → Exposed AC.
  4. The corroded patches inside the exposure are Rust, and may overlap the exposure.
  5. The painted numeral itself still needs annotating. Ruling JP-6

Glossary#

AF paintAntifouling paint — the outermost coat, which stops marine growth attaching to the hull. It is the coloured surface you usually see.
AC paintAnticorrosive paint — the layer beneath the AF coat, protecting the steel. Usually pale grey or white where exposed.
Exposed steel plateThe hull's bare steel, visible because both paint layers have gone. Blackish or metallic in tone.
Rust leachateRust that has dissolved and run out of a corroded area, staining the surface below it. The leachate is Dirt; the corroded source is Rust.
Rust streak / rust stainThe mark left where leachate ran down the hull. Heavy → Dirt 1. Light → Dirt 2.
Water scaleDeposit left behind when water dries on the surface — pale, often patchy. Classed as Dirt 2.
Normal AF wearEven, widespread thinning of the antifouling coat through ordinary use. Not dirt — it is Sound.
MaskThe output image, where each pixel carries the RGB value of its class.
GranularityHow closely your outline follows the real shape of an anomaly. See FP-4.
ToleranceThe permitted misalignment between your boundary and the true edge — 2 px on the outside. See the diagram.
SegmentationLabelling every pixel of the image with a class, rather than drawing boxes.
